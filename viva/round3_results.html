Which graphs did you plot, and what are the axes?

What does your D50 = 93.5 µm tell you?

Half the mass (by weight) is finer than about 94 µm. It is the median size of the ground ore. Most of the mass sat on the 75 and 53 µm sieves.

What does D80 = 151.6 µm mean, and why is it used?

80% of the mass passes 152 µm. It is the standard grind-size indicator for feed and product of grinding.

What is the fineness number?

Σ(cumulative grams retained) ÷ 100, from the manual. A higher value means a coarser or finer powder depending on the definition used, so just say you computed it as the manual says, and that the two trials agree (5.078 ± 0.039).

How do you know the result is reproducible?

The two trials gave similar distributions and fineness numbers (spread ±0.039). Recovered masses were close (117.32 vs 115.84 g).

Why do the cumulative oversize and undersize curves look like mirror images?

Oversize % + undersize % = 100% at every size.

What are the sources of error?
  • Particles stuck in the mesh (blinding), recovered by brushing.
  • Mass loss from the ≈120 g nominal charge (117.32 g and 115.84 g recovered).
  • Shaker amplitude fluctuated between 1.0 and 1.1 mm in trial 2 (noted in the report).
  • Sampling not perfectly representative.
What did you conclude about liberation?

Sieve analysis tells you whether grinding reached the liberation window. Finer than needed wastes energy and makes slimes. Coarser leaves locked grains.

What graphs should this experiment give?
  • Settling time (and velocity) vs particle size: time rises steeply as size falls (t ∝ 1/d²).

Why does halving the size make the settling time about four times longer?

Flow around the particle is laminar (creeping), so Stokes’ law is valid (limit Re < 0.2).

What are the sources of error?

  • Incomplete dispersion (clumps).
  • Disturbing the bed while siphoning.
  • Temperature change (viscosity).

Which graphs did you plot?
  • Calibration: absorbance (y) vs Fe concentration (x), a straight line.
  • [Fe] vs time (0–90 min): quick rise, then a flat plateau.
Describe the [Fe] vs time curve.

Concentration jumped from about 46 to 52 mg/L in the first 15 min, then stayed at about 52.5 mg/L until 90 min.

Why does it plateau?
  • The easily leachable iron (surface hematite, goethite) dissolved quickly.
  • The rest is locked in the inert aluminosilicate framework; a product layer slows acid (ash-layer diffusion).
  • Acid use and approach to equilibrium also play a part.
Say it like this: The leachable iron was extracted in the first 15 minutes; the rest is refractory.
What does the calibration curve tell you?

AAS follows Beer–Lambert: absorbance is proportional to concentration. R² = 0.9978 shows a good linear fit, so the conversion is reliable.

What does the plateau say about the rate-controlling step?

The report points to ash/product-layer diffusion control after the fast chemical attack. Shrinking core model: film diffusion, ash diffusion, or chemical reaction.

Why isn’t the concentration zero at the first reading?

Surface iron dissolved quickly once the acid was added, so by the first measurement it was already high. This is what the report calls the “initial baseline”. Say it simply if asked, and avoid over-explaining.

How could you extract more iron?

Higher temperature, stronger acid, finer grinding, more agitation, longer time, or a different acid.

Sources of error?
  • Dilution steps and pipetting.
  • 1 mL sample replaced with water (corrected).
  • AAS drift or noise (±0.35 mg/L).

Which graphs did you plot?
  • Calibration: absorbance vs concentration.
  • [Cu²⁺] vs time: falls continuously (exponential decay).
  • ln C vs t: roughly straight, slope gives k.
Describe the concentration–time curve.

[Cu²⁺] fell steadily from 25.96 to 15.07 mg/L over 90 min. The rate is fastest at the start and slows as Cu²⁺ is used up.

What order is the reaction and what does that mean?

Apparent first order: rate ∝ [Cu²⁺]. It points to boundary-layer mass transfer of Cu²⁺ to the iron surface. R² = 0.94 is moderate, so say “approximately first order”.

What is the half-life and what is it?

117 min, the time for [Cu²⁺] to halve (t½ = ln2 / k). In 90 min it did not quite halve, matching 42% removal.

Is 41.95% recovery good?

It is moderate. A longer time, higher stirring, more iron area, or a fresh iron surface would give more. The reaction is thermodynamically favourable (ΔE° = 0.78 V), but rate-limited by mass transfer.

What does the 1.138 g Cu/g Fe ratio mean?

It is the stoichiometric value 63.55 ÷ 55.85. In practice more iron is used because of Fe + 2H⁺ → Fe²⁺ + H₂ and Fe³⁺ reduction.

Compare cementation with electrowinning from your results.

Cementation: spontaneous, no electricity, slow, impure copper, iron consumed. Electrowinning: needs power, controllable with current, high purity.

At what scale is cementation practical?

Large volumes of dilute copper solution (mine waters, leach residues), with cheap scrap iron.

  • Ignore the README (5.46 g, 92.1%). It does not match your report or the experiment.
  • By Faraday, 100 mA × 10 min gives only 0.0198 g (and 0.0396 g at 200 mA). That would make efficiency about 110% and 207%, not 73.65% and 138.28%. The reported figures imply about 15 min of charge. Check the actual time in your notebook before you quote percentages.

Which graphs did you plot?
  • Current and voltage vs time for each cathode. Noisy at the start while electrodes were settling into position.
  • Mass of Cu recovered per cathode.
  • Cost per g for each cathode.
Why is the current efficiency above 100% for the larger cathode?

Impossible physically, so it is an error. Your report explains it: the copper deposit grew out and bridged to the other electrode, which raised the current and deposited extra copper not counted in the Faraday calculation.

Say it like this: The deposit bridged between the electrodes, so the real charge exceeded what we assumed. The percentage is not reliable.
Why is the efficiency 73.65% for the small one, and what loses current?

Efficiency below 100% is normal: H₂ evolution, loose deposit falling off, brass dissolving, weighing error.

What was your conclusion?

More copper than theory was recovered because of bridging, so errors are large. You can’t conclude whether the smaller or larger electrode is more economical.

What is the formula for the theoretical mass?

m = M·I·t / (n·F), with M = 63.55 g/mol, n = 2, F = 96485 C/mol.

How did you get the energy and cost?

E = ∫V·I dt (sum of V·I·Δt from the logged data) in kWh, then cost per g = E × tariff ÷ Δm, tariff 6.8 INR/kWh.

How would you improve the experiment?
  • Stir the bath and keep the deposit away from the other electrode.
  • Use a reference (stable) electrode position and fixed spacing.
  • Repeat each run.
  • Use a higher Cu concentration.
What is the minimum Cu concentration for recovery?

At low concentration, the limiting current falls below the applied current, so H⁺ is reduced and efficiency collapses. Our dilute bath (about 2.5 g/L Cu from 10 g/L CuSO₄·5H₂O) is already below industrial values (30–50 g/L).

Which graphs are in your report?
  • Carothers curve: degree of polymerization vs conversion. Flat at first, shoots up near 100% conversion.
  • A schematic of the liquid–liquid interface. Both are theory, not measured data.
What does the Carothers curve show?

X̄ₙ = 1 / (1 − p). At 90% conversion X̄ₙ = 10, at 99% it is 100, at 99.9% it is 1000. High molecular weight needs very high conversion, which is why step-growth needs pure monomers and exact stoichiometry.

Which is the limiting reagent, and how do you know?

The diamine: 8.605 mmol vs 15.30 mmol of acid chloride. The 1:1 reaction runs out of diamine first.

How was the theoretical yield calculated?

8.605 mmol × 226.32 g/mol (repeat unit) = 1.9475 g.

What is your percent yield?

Not measured. Say: “It was a demonstration, so I only have the theoretical yield, 1.9475 g. From the mechanism I expect about 50–70%.”

Why would the real yield be below theoretical?
  • Fixed interfacial area; the rope parts before monomer is used up.
  • Short chains stay dissolved.
  • Some amine is protonated by HCl.
  • Losses on washing and drying.
Was the method effective?

Yes: a continuous nylon rope could be drawn from the interface in minutes at room temperature. This showed interfacial step-growth polymerization. Effectiveness in yield was not measured.
