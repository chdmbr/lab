2Linking questions: the big picture

Examiners like to ask how the experiments fit together.

How do your six experiments connect?

Five form one metal extraction story: sieve (size control) → sedimentation (finer size separation) → leaching (dissolve the metal) → cementation (cheap recovery) → electrowinning (pure recovery). Nylon is the separate polymer processing experiment.

Why does particle size matter for leaching?

Smaller particles have more surface area, so leaching is faster. Sieve analysis tells you whether the ore is ground to the right size.

What do leaching and cementation have in common?

Both use AAS and sampling at 15 min intervals. Both are controlled by diffusion/mass transfer and follow similar kinetic analysis.

Cementation vs electrowinning: when would you use which?

Cementation for large volumes of dilute solutions with cheap scrap iron (impure copper). Electrowinning for concentrated clean solutions where you need 99.99% copper.

What would change if the leach solution had other metals?

They could co-deposit or consume iron/current. Industry uses solvent extraction first (SX–EW) to get a clean concentrated copper electrolyte.

How could each process scale up?
  • Leaching: heaps or stirred tanks, controlled temperature and acid use.
  • Cementation: launders or cones with scrap iron.
  • Electrowinning: many large cathodes in a tankhouse, 45–50 °C, 30–50 g/L Cu.
  • Nylon: continuous interfacial or melt processes.
Where does sustainability come in?

Recycling: e-waste and scrap are rich in Cu, Al and Fe; recycled copper saves about 85% energy and recycled aluminium about 95% (figures from the manual).

Why is diffusion important in all these?

Leaching (film/ash diffusion), cementation (boundary layer), electrowinning (limiting current), nylon (monomers reaching the interface), sedimentation (viscous drag).

3Trap and honesty questions

Where examiners press. Know the honest, correct answer.

Rule: if you didn’t measure it, say so. Quote only numbers from your own notebook and submitted reports.
“What was your d50 in sedimentation?”

The four fraction weighings were not transcribed, so I don’t have a measured d50. I can give the method, the Stokes settling times (2.11, 3.30, 5.87, 13.21 min at 8 cm), and Re = 0.018.

“What was your nylon yield?”

It was a demonstration; I didn’t weigh the product. Theoretical yield is 1.9475 g from the limiting diamine. I’d expect roughly 50–70% from the mechanism.

“Your electrowinning efficiency is over 100%. How?”

It can’t be. The copper deposit grew out and bridged to the other electrode, raising the current, so our Faraday calculation was off. That is why we couldn’t conclude which cathode was more economical.

“Your calibration is linear. Why does that matter?”

AAS obeys Beer–Lambert in a range. A linear calibration (R² ≈ 0.998) means absorbance converts reliably to concentration within 0–25 mg/L, so samples must be diluted into that range.

“Why isn’t the [Fe] zero at the start?”

Surface iron dissolved quickly once the acid was added, so the first measurement was already high. Don’t over-explain.

“Is the cementation really first order?”

Approximately: R² = 0.94 is moderate. It is consistent with mass-transfer control, but I’d not claim it is exactly first order.

“Which is better, the 2×5 or 4×5 cathode?”

Our data can’t decide; bridging caused large errors. Theory says a larger area lowers current density, giving a smoother deposit.

“Can Stokes’ law be applied to your quartz?”

Yes, approximately: Re = 0.018 ≪ 0.2 and the slurry was dilute. Real quartz grains aren’t perfect spheres, so sizes are “equivalent Stokes diameters”.

“Can sieving size a 5 µm powder?”

No: below ≈ 38 µm blinding and agglomeration dominate. Use sedimentation or laser methods.

4Mixed bag: any experiment

Random. Shuffle yourself: cover the answers and go.

Why must the cathode area be known in electrowinning?

To get current density and compare cathodes fairly.

Why does a small particle settle slowly?

Terminal velocity ∝ d², and viscous drag dominates for small particles.

Why replace the 1 mL sample with water?

To keep the volume constant.

What does a plateau on the leaching curve mean?

No more leachable iron, acid depleted, or product-layer limited.

Why a large beaker for nylon?

To avoid splashing reactive chemicals.

What is the 1.138 in cementation?

Stoichiometric g Cu per g Fe (63.55 ÷ 55.85).

Why add NaOH, in one line?

To neutralise HCl so the amine stays reactive.

Why do we dilute AAS samples?

To bring the concentration into the instrument’s linear range.

What is hindered settling?

Concentrated slurry where upward liquid flow and collisions slow particles.

What is Nylon 6 made from?

Caprolactam by ring-opening polymerization.

Why is the iron plate cleaned before cementation?

To remove oxide so iron contacts the solution.

Which two things limit electrowinning at low Cu concentration?

Limiting current (mass transport) and hydrogen evolution.
