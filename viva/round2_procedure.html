1Sieve analysis

Pb–Zn ore, vibratory shaker, ASTM E11 / ISO 3310.

Pb–Zn ore powderSieve set 250/150/105/75/53/37 µm + panVibratory sieve shaker (1.1 mm, 10 min)BalanceBrush
Walk me through the procedure in order.
  • Mix the powder thoroughly.
  • Cone and quarter to reduce the sample.
  • Weigh the sample.
  • Stack sieves coarsest on top, pan at the bottom; load the sample on top.
  • Shake 10–15 min.
  • Weigh each fraction and the pan.
  • Calculate % retained, cumulative oversize and undersize, then plot.
Why cone and quarter? How is it done?

It cuts a big sample down to a small one that is still representative. Spread, mix, pile into a cone, flatten, divide into 4, discard opposite diagonals, repeat.

Say it like this: Cone and quarter reduces the sample size without biasing the size distribution.
Why is the coarsest sieve on top?
  • Stepwise classification: coarse particles are caught first.
  • Protects the delicate fine meshes from heavy load.
  • Prevents blinding of fine sieves.
Why a fixed shaking time (10–15 min)?

Long enough that nothing more passes (completion), and the same for every run so results compare. Too long breaks particles (attrition).

Why weigh everything again at the end?

Mass balance. Sum of fractions should be close to the starting mass. Loss shows spillage or particles stuck in the mesh. Our two trials recovered 117.32 g and 115.84 g.

Why two trials?

To check reproducibility. Similar fineness number in both trials shows the method is repeatable.

Why brush the sieve gently from the back?

To recover near-size particles wedged in the holes (blinding) without damaging the wires.

2Sedimentation and decantation

Quartz powder, sodium oxalate dispersant, siphon at 8 cm.

Quartz powder (ρ = 2.65 g/cm³)5 L vessel (2 L beaker)Sodium oxalate 2 g / 5 L10 mm siphon, upturned intakeStopwatchFilter paper + vacuum pumpOven (80 °C)
Walk me through the procedure.
  • Make sodium oxalate solution (2 g per 5 L water).
  • Put quartz in the vessel, fill with the solution. Wet fully, no clumps (ultrasound if needed).
  • Put the siphon 8 cm below the surface.
  • Wait the Stokes settling time, then siphon off the supernatant.
  • Repeat settle and decant for the next size.
  • Filter, dry in the oven, weigh each fraction.
Why add sodium oxalate?

It is a dispersant. It adsorbs on quartz, gives particles the same negative charge, so they repel and don’t clump. Clumps settle faster and give wrong sizes.

Why ensure full wetting and use ultrasound?

To break up agglomerates so that Stokes’ law is applied to single particles.

Why siphon at exactly 8 cm?

8 cm is the settling distance h. Settling time = h ÷ terminal velocity. After that time, particles bigger than the cut size are below the siphon tip.

Say it like this: h is fixed, so each settling time corresponds to one cut size.
Why an upturned siphon, and why not disturb the sediment?

It draws liquid from above its tip with no downward suction. Stirring the bed would carry settled coarse particles into the fines.

Why repeat settle and decant?

One pass leaves fines trapped in the sediment. Repeating washes them out.

Why filter paper, vacuum, oven, and tare weighing?

Vacuum filtering separates solids quickly, the oven dries them (no water weight), and subtracting the empty paper’s mass gives the net dry mass of each fraction.

Why must temperature stay constant?

Viscosity of water changes with temperature, and Stokes’ law uses viscosity. It is one of the Stokes assumptions.

3Leaching of bauxite (iron removal)

Bauxite + dilute HCl, 70 °C, sampled for 90 min, measured by AAS.

Bauxite ore 2 g (passing 75 µm)HCl: 20 mL + 80 mL waterConical flaskIncubator shaker, 70 °CMicropipette + tipsEppendorf tubesAAS (Fe, 248.3 nm)
Walk me through the procedure.
  • Weigh 2 g bauxite.
  • Make 100 mL of acid: 20 mL HCl + 80 mL water. Add to ore in the flask.
  • Keep in the incubator shaker at 70 °C.
  • Every 15 min for 90 min, take 1 mL from the top, then add 1 mL water.
  • Dilute the samples and measure Fe by AAS.
Why is the ore ground fine first?

Smaller particles have more surface area per gram, so more is in contact with the acid and leaching is faster.

Why 70 °C and a shaker?

Heat speeds up the reaction and diffusion. The shaker thins the liquid film around particles and keeps temperature and mixing uniform.

Why add acid to water (20 mL acid into 80 mL water)?

To get a dilute, controllable solution safely. Always acid into water, never the reverse.

Why replace the 1 mL with water?

To keep the volume constant so concentrations stay comparable. The dilution is then corrected for in the data.

Why sample from the top?

To take clear liquid rather than settled ore particles.

Why dilute the samples before AAS?

Concentration may be above the AAS linear range (we calibrated 0–25 mg/L). Dilution brings it into range.

Why a calibration curve?

AAS reads absorbance. Standards of known concentration give absorbance vs concentration, and you read unknowns off it.

Why micropipette and Eppendorf tubes?

Small, accurate volumes (1 mL) for timed sampling and dilution.

4Cementation of copper

Fe plate in dilute CuSO₄, stirred, sampled for 90 min, AAS for Cu.

500 mL deionised water500 mg CuSO₄·5H₂O1–2 drops 10 N H₂SO₄ (pH ≈ 2)Clean iron plateMagnetic stirrer + beadPipettes, Eppendorf tubesAAS (Cu, 324.7 nm)
Walk me through the procedure.
  • Beaker: 500 mL DI water + 1–2 drops 10 N H₂SO₄ + 500 mg copper sulphate.
  • Stir with the magnetic bead; insert the clean iron plate.
  • Take 1 mL every 15 min from t = 0 to 90 min, replacing each with 1 mL water.
  • Dilute with pH-2 water; run AAS with standard Cu solutions.
Why a few drops of acid?

Better conductivity and a clean iron surface (no hydroxide films). Only a little, because iron also reacts with acid (Fe + 2H⁺ → Fe²⁺ + H₂), wasting iron.

Why clean/pickle the iron plate?

To remove oxide or scale so iron actually touches the solution.

Why stir?

It thins the diffusion layer, so Cu²⁺ reaches the iron faster. The process is mass-transfer controlled.

Why sample from t = 0 to 90 min at 15 min?

To follow how Cu²⁺ concentration falls with time, which gives the kinetics.

Why dilute with pH-2 water?

It keeps the sample at the same acidity as the bath so the copper stays dissolved, and brings it into the AAS range.

What does the standard-solution calibration do?

Converts absorbance into mg/L of copper (Cu lamp at 324.7 nm).

What else do you measure to compare Cu and Fe?

Copper removed against iron consumed (plate mass loss). The stoichiometric ratio is 1.138 g Cu per g Fe (63.55 ÷ 55.85).

5Electrowinning of copper

Brass cathode, inert Ir anode, constant current, 10 min.

200 mL bath: 10 g/L CuSO₄·5H₂O + 0.1 M H₂SO₄ (our bath)Brass cathodes 2×5 and 4×5 cmIr (inert) anodeDC power supplyBalance, oven
Walk me through the procedure.
  • Make 200 mL of your assigned bath.
  • Cut two brass cathodes; wash with soapy water, rinse with DI, dry briefly; weigh (note balance precision).
  • Mark half the length (2.5 cm).
  • Brass to the cathode (working) terminal, inert electrode to the anode.
  • Constant current 10 min: 100 mA (2×5 cm), 200 mA (4×5 cm).
  • Dry the cathode 1–2 min, re-weigh.
  • Keep leftover electrolyte for AAS.
Why wash and dry the cathode before weighing?

Grease or dirt blocks plating and changes the mass. Dry so the mass is not water weight.

Why dry the plated cathode only 1–2 min?

Enough to drive off water, but long drying could oxidise or change the deposit.

Why a mark at 2.5 cm?

A fixed immersion depth gives a known plated area. Current density is known, and cathodes and runs compare fairly. The clip stays out of the liquid.

Why constant current (galvanostatic)?

Charge = I × t is known, so you can compare the actual mass with Faraday’s law.

Why 100 mA and 200 mA for the two cathodes?

Larger cathode, larger current, so the current density is kept similar and only the size effect is tested.

Why add 0.1 M H₂SO₄?

It makes the solution conductive and keeps Cu²⁺ from hydrolysing.

Why save the leftover electrolyte?

AAS on it shows how much Cu is left, which is needed to find the minimum recoverable concentration.

How does your bath differ from a real tankhouse?

Real: about 30–50 g/L Cu, 150–180 g/L H₂SO₄, 45–50 °C, stirred or circulated, 200–400 A/m². Ours: dilute, room temperature, unstirred, 200 mL.

Safety?

The currents are low, but keep hands out of the bath. About 10 mA through the body can damage tissue. Wear goggles, gloves and a coat.

6Nylon 6,6 synthesis

Interfacial polycondensation: diamine in water, acid chloride in hexane.

1 g diaminohexane → 25 mL water0.47 g NaOH2.5 mL (2.8 g) adipoyl chloride → 25 mL hexane100 mL (or bigger) beakerGlass rod, tweezers45–50 °C water bath
Walk me through the procedure.
  • Warm the diamine bottle in a 45–50 °C bath (it melts at 42 °C); pipette 1 g into a volumetric flask; make up to 25 mL with water.
  • Add 0.47 g NaOH.
  • Dissolve 2.5 mL adipoyl chloride in hexane to 25 mL.
  • Pour the aqueous solution into a large beaker.
  • Gently pour the hexane layer over it (down a glass rod).
  • A film forms at the interface. Pull it out with tweezers, wrap it on a rod and keep pulling.
  • Wash, dry, weigh.
Why warm the diamine?

Its melting point is 42 °C. It is a hard solid, so melting makes it easy to transfer and weigh.

Why NaOH?

It neutralises the HCl made in the reaction. Otherwise the amine is protonated (–NH₃⁺) and stops reacting.

Why hexane for the acid chloride?

It dissolves adipoyl chloride, doesn’t mix with water (needed for an interface), and doesn’t react with it. Water would destroy the acid chloride.

Why pour the second layer gently?

Mixing would make the reaction happen throughout, giving lumps, not a continuous film at the interface.

Why a large beaker?

To avoid splash hazards with reactive chemicals.

Why wash the nylon before weighing?

To remove leftover monomers, NaOH, NaCl and HCl so they are not counted as polymer.

Safety?

Adipoyl chloride is very reactive (reacts with moisture, gives HCl fumes). Use a hood, gloves and goggles. Hexane is flammable.
