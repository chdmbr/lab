Two warnings if you use these. (1) The copper never broke — it folded into a V and stayed in one piece, so 212 J is a lower bound on its toughness, not a fracture energy. (2) The stainless steel's electronic display read 299.86 J, which is the full 300 J swing — a machine fault. The dial read 107 J and that is the real figure.

Fracture surfaces

All six EN-8 halves: flat and shiny, 100 % cleavage, 0 % shear, no lateral expansion, no shear lips — the three heat treatments were indistinguishable by eye. Stainless steel: dull, fibrous, chevron markings, shear lips. Copper: did not separate.

The quenched face scattered 43–50 HRC across repeat readings; 50 is quoted. That face is scaled, unground and not square to the indenter, and every one of those faults pushes a Rockwell reading down, never up — so the spread is one-sided and the highest reading is the best estimate. It is also closest to the 55–57 HRC expected of fully martensitic 0.4 wt% C steel. ASTM A255 starts its traverse at 1.6 mm anyway, so the 0 mm point is excluded from any fit.

The headline result. Water-quenched EN-8 reaches only 23–27 HRC a millimetre or two below the surface — the Jominy bar and the water-quenched Charpy bar (255.5 HV ≈ 23 HRC) agree independently. EN-8 does not through-harden.
